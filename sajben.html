STAR-CCM+ · Verification & Validation

Sajben Transonic Diffuser: Separating Numerical Error from Model Error

Objective
Solve a compressible internal flow with a shock, bound the numerical error, then characterize where RANS departs from experiment.
Tools
Simcenter STAR-CCM+ 2210, Python.
Case
Sajben transonic diffuser, weak-shock condition. NASA WIND (NPARC Alliance) validation archive.
Role
Built the geometry, mesh family, and case setup; ran all cases; wrote the post-processing and V&V analysis.
Result
Grid-converged shock position 1.4230 x/h with numerical uncertainty (GCI) of 0.22%, against an experimental 1.280. That is a +11.2% modelling error, roughly 50x larger than the discretisation uncertainty. Turbulence closure accounts for about half of it.

Problem

When a CFD result disagrees with experiment, there are two possible causes. Either the equations were solved imprecisely, meaning the mesh was too coarse, or the wrong equations were solved, meaning the physical model is inadequate. Most studies cannot separate these, which makes a statement like "my result is 10% off" close to uninterpretable. The disagreement might vanish on a finer mesh, or it might be the physics, and without further work there is no way to tell which.

This study separates them. Three systematically refined meshes bound the numerical contribution to a quantified uncertainty. Once that bound exists, whatever disagreement remains is attributable to the model. This is standard practice in industrial verification and validation, and it is the step most commonly skipped.

The case is relevant to propulsion as an analogue for off-nominal, overexpanded nozzle flow: a shock sitting inside the duct with shock-induced separation, as occurs at low altitude or during throttling. It is deliberately not the clean isentropic expansion of a nozzle at its design point.

Verification and validation are different questions

Verification asks: am I solving the equations right? This is pure mathematics and has nothing to do with reality. The RANS equations have an exact solution on an infinitely fine mesh. Any real mesh is finite, so the answer carries a discretisation error. Verification measures that error. It could be done without ever looking at experimental data.

Validation asks: am I solving the right equations? This compares against physical measurement. Whatever disagreement remains after the numerical error has been bounded is attributable to the model itself: the turbulence closure, the 2D assumption, the steady assumption.

The order matters. You cannot honestly claim RANS is wrong by 11% until you have shown that mesh error is much smaller than 11%.

The case

The Sajben diffuser is a converging-diverging duct that is one-sided: the lower wall is flat across the entire domain and all area variation is in the upper wall. That asymmetry is why the experiment reports upper and lower wall pressures separately, and it provides two independent validation curves from a single run.

Air enters subsonically at M = 0.46, accelerates through the contraction to about M = 1.25 just past the throat, terminates in a normal shock, separates from the upper wall, and recovers subsonically through the diffuser.

What makes it a hard case is that the shock and the boundary layer are coupled. The shock's pressure rise thickens and separates the boundary layer, the separated layer changes the effective flow area, and the effective area determines where the shock sits. RANS turbulence models handle this coupling imperfectly, and the characteristic failure, a shock predicted slightly downstream of the measured position, appears across every code in the archive.

Setup

Geometry

Wall coordinates come from the archive as 81 points per wall. Connecting those points with straight segments produces a wall that deviates from a smooth curve by up to 350 um at worst, in the high-curvature part of the contraction. The finest first-cell height in the mesh family is 1.09 um, so the faceting error would be roughly 320 times the first cell height, and every facet corner would act as a spurious weak compression polluting the boundary layer the mesh was built to resolve. Both walls were therefore resampled onto a cubic spline at 801 points before meshing.

The resampled geometry was checked against the archive specification: throat height 1.732199 in against 1.73221 in, and exit-to-throat area ratio 1.5000 against 1.5.

This is an assumption worth stating plainly. The cubic spline is a reconstruction of the wall between NASA's points, and the true tunnel contour is not available. A spline is the standard choice for a smooth physical wall and is far closer to reality than piecewise-linear segments, but it is not the original geometry.

Boundary conditions

The inflow is subsonic, so the inlet state is not determined by upstream values alone: downstream pressure information propagates back to it. A stagnation inlet imposes P0 and T0 and lets the boundary compute velocity from the interior solution, which is the physically correct treatment for a reservoir-driven subsonic inflow. A velocity inlet would hard-code the quantity being predicted.

That choice produces a free verification check. Mach number was never imposed anywhere in the setup, and it converged to 0.4600 against the published 0.46, an error of 0.01%. For that to happen, the geometry, the boundary conditions, and the gas model all have to be mutually consistent. A geometry error, a unit error, or a wrong reference pressure would show up here.

A second assumption: the archive does not specify inlet turbulence quantities, so solver defaults were retained (turbulence intensity 1%, turbulent viscosity ratio 10). Using the default is more defensible than a hand-picked value when nothing is sourced.

Mesh

Structured mesh built with STAR-CCM+ Directed Mesh, three levels, refinement ratio exactly r = 1.5 in both directions.

The identical near-wall aspect ratio across all three meshes is the quantitative proof of geometric self-similarity, which is the condition Richardson extrapolation requires. Both cell dimensions scale by exactly 1/1.5 per level, so cell shape is preserved and only cell size changes. Most studies assert self-similarity; here it is a number that demonstrates it.

Worth noting that y+ is non-monotonic across the family (0.998, 0.866, 0.933) even though cell heights shrink monotonically. y+ depends on computed wall shear as well as spacing, and better boundary-layer resolution produces higher computed shear, which partly offsets the finer cells. All three meshes are wall-resolved regardless. y+ had been predicted at 2.0 from a flat-plate correlation and came out near 1.0, because the correlation overestimates wall shear at the throat where it does not account for the favourable pressure gradient of accelerating flow.

Why structured, and why r = 1.5 rather than the more common root-2

Structured because GCI requires geometrically self-similar grids with an unambiguous refinement ratio. Scaling node count per direction shrinks every cell by the same factor, so the representative cell size is well defined. On an unstructured mesh, "refine by 1.5x" is fuzzy: the mesher redistributes cells non-uniformly and the effective ratio varies spatially. A secondary reason is shock capture. Quad faces aligned with the flow keep the shock crisp, whereas a shock cutting diagonally across skewed cells is smeared over a wider band, which blurs the shock position, the headline measured quantity.

r = 1.5 because the closed-form observed-order formula assumes a constant refinement ratio, and root-2 cannot be hit exactly with integer node counts. Going 160, 226, 320 gives actual ratios of 1.4125 and 1.4159, which drift. Using 160, 240, 360 gives exactly 1.5 at every level, so the closed form is legitimately valid as written. It also produces larger grid separation, which gives a cleaner signal for the observed order against iterative noise.

Solver

Coupled density-based implicit solver, chosen because the entire problem is a shock and a density-based scheme handles the tight pressure-density-velocity coupling across a discontinuity. Roe flux-difference splitting, second-order discretisation throughout, Sutherland viscosity, and low-Mach preconditioning, which is necessary because the flow spans M = 0.46 to above M = 1.

Solver defaults were retained until they demonstrably failed. The automatic CFL controller ramped to about 17,000, at which point the linear solver began rejecting solves and repeatedly halving CFL, and residuals stopped converging. Capping maximum CFL at 50 dropped all residuals by roughly an order of magnitude and stabilised the run. This was a documented intervention after an observed failure rather than pre-emptive tuning, and CFL affects only the path to convergence and not the converged answer, since the pseudo-time terms vanish at steady state.

Verification

This section comes before validation, and the ordering is the point.

Iterative convergence

Mass is conserved to 2.8 × 10-5 % on the fine mesh, with residuals flat. Mass flow also sits at 99.08% of the ideal choked value, and that 0.9% deficit is physically correct rather than an error: the boundary layer occupies part of the throat and reduces the effective area below the geometric area. A result of exactly 100% would indicate the boundary layer was missing.

One methodological correction is worth reporting. The protocol initially specified a fixed 5000 iterations on every mesh for comparability. That was insufficient for the fine mesh, which was still showing residual excursions and needed 6500. Identical iteration count does not mean identical convergence state, and Richardson extrapolation assumes iterative error is negligible against discretisation error. The defensible criterion is a convergence level applied per mesh, with iteration count reported as an outcome rather than a setting.

The coarse mesh settled into a limit cycle rather than converging. Refinement eliminated it entirely, which identified it as numerical shock jitter, the shock oscillating between adjacent cell faces on a mesh whose streamwise cells were 0.079 h, rather than the physical self-excited unsteadiness this diffuser is known for. Distinguishing the two mattered: the coarse mesh showed constant-amplitude oscillation with no downward trend, where more iterations would not have helped, while the fine mesh showed decaying excursions on a downward trend, where they did.

Grid convergence

Shock position leads this section. It is the quantity the experiment measures, it is the documented RANS failure mode for this case, and it is the engineering-relevant result, since in a nozzle the shock location determines where the flow separates.

Across the family on the upper wall, shock position went 1.3719, 1.4117, 1.4205, extrapolating to 1.4230. The increments shrank from +0.0398 to +0.0088 h, a ratio of 0.22, which is the asymptotic behaviour the method requires. If the increments were not shrinking, the solution would not be converging.

The three formulas, with a worked example

Richardson extrapolation assumes the computed answer approaches the exact one as a power law in cell size: f(h) = f_exact + C·h^p. There are three unknowns (f_exact, C, and p), which is why the study uses three meshes. 

Observed order:     p = ln( |(f3 - f2)/(f2 - f1)| ) / ln(r)
Extrapolated:       f_exact = f1 + (f1 - f2)/(r^p - 1)
Uncertainty:        GCI_fine = Fs * |(f2 - f1)/f1| / (r^p - 1)
Asymptotic check:   R = GCI_32 / (r^p * GCI_21),  should be near 1

f1 is the fine mesh, f2 medium, f3 coarse. r is the refinement ratio, defined on cell edge length rather than cell count. Fs is a safety factor, 1.25 for a three-grid study.

Worked through for shock position on the upper wall:

f3 = 1.3719, f2 = 1.4117, f1 = 1.4205
(f3 - f2) = -0.0398,  (f2 - f1) = -0.0088
ratio = 4.5227
p = ln(4.5227)/ln(1.5) = 3.7221

r^p = 1.5^3.7221 = 4.5193
f_exact = 1.4205 + 0.0088/3.5193 = 1.4230

GCI = 1.25 * (0.0088/1.4205) / 3.5193 = 0.00220 = 0.220%

GCI is an uncertainty band rather than an error. It states that the true grid-independent value lies within 0.220% of the fine-mesh result, with the safety factor making it deliberately conservative.

The discontinuity caveat, and why the data resolves it

NASA's V&V guidance for this archive states that where shocks and other discontinuities are present, Richardson extrapolation is invalid in the region of the discontinuity, though it is still felt to apply to solution functionals computed from the entire flow field. The reason is that Richardson rests on a Taylor series expansion, and a Taylor series does not exist across a discontinuity.

The caveat is specifically about applying Richardson pointwise, extrapolating a field value at a grid point inside the shock. Shock position is not a pointwise field value. It is a derived scalar, the location of a feature extracted from the entire wall pressure distribution, which places it on the solution-functional side of that distinction.

The data demonstrates exactly that split, and it is worth presenting as a finding rather than a footnote:

The quantities that failed to converge are precisely the pointwise-at-the-shock values the caveat concerns, and the quantities that converged are the ones it exempts. The theoretical limit was not assumed, it was observed. Three separate quantities evaluated inside the discontinuity all refused to produce a valid GCI, while feature-location and smooth-region quantities converged cleanly with asymptotic ratios within 0.6% of unity.

The empirical check is the decisive argument here. NASA prescribes the asymptotic ratio as the test of whether the power law actually holds across the family. Shock position gives 1.006, and their own worked example describes 1.002 as well within the asymptotic range. So the theory raises a caution, the prescribed check passes, and the quantities theory says should fail did fail.

One honest qualification: separation bubble length is also a feature measure and it also oscillated, so the pointwise-versus-feature distinction is not a clean law. That case has a simpler explanation, which is that the bubble is only one to two cells wide on every mesh in the family, so it is under-resolved rather than mathematically excluded.

Three distinct convergence behaviours

The observed order came out differently for different quantities, and each difference has a physical explanation.

Smooth field quantities converge near formal second order. Peak Mach gives p = 2.39 against a formal 2, which is the baseline the others should be read against.

The discontinuity-located quantity is anomalous but well behaved. Shock position gives p = 3.72, above formal order. A captured discontinuity snaps toward its converged location faster than smooth-error theory predicts, and the asymptotic ratio validates the extrapolation regardless.

Boundary-layer-driven quantities converge slowly. Mass flow and inlet Mach give p near 0.5, because both are set by displacement thickness at the throat and boundary layers resolve more slowly than the inviscid core. Their GCIs remain small, so practical uncertainty is fine, but second-order behaviour cannot be claimed for them.

The general lesson is that observed order is a property of the quantity, not just of the solver.

The error is positional, not amplitude. The pressure jump has the correct magnitude, it simply happens about 0.14 throat-heights too far downstream. Downstream diffuser recovery is captured to 0.26%, which is essentially exact, and the regional split turns a single number into a diagnosis: the model is nearly perfect where the flow is well behaved and fails in one specific place.

Global RMS increases with refinement, going 4.36, 4.84, 4.86% on the top wall. Out of context that looks like a defect. It is the study's central evidence. A coarse mesh smears the shock, which accidentally overlaps the experimental data slightly better, and sharpening the shock reveals the true model error. This trend should never be presented without the regional breakdown, which shows upstream and downstream errors flat or improving throughout.

Why the shock sits downstream

Shock position in a duct is set by the balance between effective flow area, meaning geometric area minus boundary layer displacement thickness, and back pressure. Under-predicting displacement thickness makes the effective area too large, so the flow expands further before it must shock.

Four modelling assumptions all bias in the same direction. The RANS eddy-viscosity closure ties turbulent stress to local mean strain and presumes local equilibrium, but through a shock the compression is rapid and turbulence lags, so the model over-predicts mixing in the interaction and keeps the boundary layer too thin and too resistant to separation. The 2D assumption neglects sidewall boundary layers, and the tunnel sidewalls were about four throat-heights apart, each carrying a boundary layer contributing blockage the model cannot represent. The uniform inflow prescribed by the archive means boundary layers grow from zero over the inlet-to-throat run and are thinner at the throat than in the experiment. And a steady solution is not the same quantity as the time-mean of the self-excited shock oscillation the experiment measured.

The sensitivity involved is worth quantifying. Placing the shock at the experimental station requires the effective area at x/h = 1.41 to be smaller by 0.330 mm, which is a displacement thickness larger by about 0.165 mm per wall, or 0.375% of throat height per wall. A sub-0.2 mm deficit per wall produces a 10% shock position error, because the area variation near the throat is gentle. That single number explains why every RANS code in the archive shows the same downstream bias.

This remains an inference. All four experimental velocity profile stations are downstream of the shock, so pre-shock displacement thickness cannot be measured directly from the available data.

SA places the shock 0.1416 h further downstream than SST, and the difference is confirmed on both the medium and fine meshes (+0.1062 h and +0.1416 h). That exceeds the numerical uncertainty of either model by an order of magnitude, so the difference is real rather than grid scatter. The gap is also roughly the size of SST's own remaining error against experiment, which means turbulence closure alone accounts for about half the total discrepancy.

An internal consistency check supports the comparison: upstream errors are essentially identical between the models, 3.238% against 3.248% on the top wall. That region is inviscid-dominated, so closure should not matter there, and it does not. The two runs differ only in the closure.

There is an apparent paradox in the separation result that is worth resolving rather than leaving. SA produces a bubble almost three times longer than SST yet places the shock further downstream, where the naive expectation is that more separation means more blockage and therefore an upstream shock. The resolution is sequencing. SA under-predicts boundary layer thickening in the accelerating pre-shock region, because it lacks SST's shear-stress-transport limiter capping eddy viscosity in adverse pressure gradients. Lower pre-shock blockage means a larger effective area, so the flow expands further before shocking, reaching peak Mach 1.280 against SST's 1.252, and the shock lands downstream at greater strength. The larger bubble is a consequence of that stronger shock, not the cause of its position.

The velocity profiles produced the more interesting result. At x/h = 1.729 the experiment and SST are both subsonic, at M = 0.924 and 0.905. SA is still supersonic at M = 1.278. It is not merely less accurate at this station, it is qualitatively wrong: it predicts supersonic flow where the experiment measured subsonic.

That leads to the methodological point of the whole study. Wall pressure alone showed SA as quantitatively worse. The second validation quantity showed it was structurally wrong. Validating against one quantity is not validation.

Reported honestly, SA's failure is concentrated at station 1. At the three downstream stations SA is marginally better than SST. The overall verdict that SST is the better model holds on wall pressure and at station 1, but SA is not uniformly worse.

Conclusion

Numerical uncertainty was bounded at 0.22%. The disagreement with experiment is 11.2% and it grows with refinement, so it is modelling error rather than discretisation error. Turbulence closure accounts for roughly half of it. SST outperforms SA on wall pressure and dramatically at the first measurement station, though SA is marginally better at the three downstream stations.

Steady RANS on a flow with known self-excited oscillations. A deliberate modelling choice. The coarse-mesh limit cycle turned out to be numerical shock jitter, eliminated by refinement, so the steady assumption was not violated at the resolutions used.

2D, no sidewall effects. The archive itself attributes downstream velocity profile disagreement to 3D effects.

Uniform inflow with no incoming boundary layer. Inherited from the case definition rather than chosen, and a leading suspect for part of the shock offset.

The separation bubble is not grid-converged. Length oscillates across the family (0.092, 0.112, 0.106 h) so no valid GCI exists, and it is reported as a range. Separation onset is more stable, between 1.558 and 1.565 h, and can be reported with more confidence.

SA converges about 5x worse than SST, with medium-to-fine shock movement of +0.0442 h against +0.0088 h. Only two SA meshes were run, so no SA GCI exists, and SA's numerical uncertainty is estimated at roughly 1% rather than SST's 0.22%. The model comparison survives comfortably, but this should not be hidden.

Odd-even decoupling appears on the fine mesh. Core velocity alternates between adjacent cells at about 2% amplitude. Counterintuitively it is absent on the medium mesh and appears with refinement, as numerical dissipation falls below what is needed to damp the mode. The gradient limiter (Venkatakrishnan, acceptable field variation 0.05) is active and correctly configured, and the oscillation sits below its activation threshold by design. Wall quantities and the shock-position GCI are unaffected.

The displacement-thickness mechanism is inferred, not measured, because all experimental profiles are downstream of the shock. Both models are also biased low downstream by 15 to 20 m/s, opposite in sign to the pre-shock deficit the mechanism requires.

Richardson extrapolation is formally invalid pointwise within a discontinuity. Shock position is a feature location derived from the full wall distribution rather than a pointwise value inside the shock, and the asymptotic ratio of 1.006 confirms the power law holds. Peak Mach (p = 2.39, GCI 0.089%, a smooth-region quantity) is reported alongside as corroboration. The three quantities that failed to converge are exactly the pointwise-at-the-shock values the caveat concerns.
